Why does this become a capacitor?
The widened depletion region has very few mobile carriers, so it acts as the dielectric insulator. The P and N regions act as the conductive plates. Together, they form a voltage-controlled capacitor — a varactor.
P plate · depletion dielectric · N plate → Cj(VR)

Why is a PN junction a capacitor?

Three ideas are enough to explain the device.

1 · Charges are separated

Under reverse bias, mobile carriers move away from the junction, leaving a depleted region with very few mobile carriers.

2 · The depletion region acts like an insulator

The P and N sides contain separated charge, while the depletion region behaves like the dielectric between capacitor plates.

3 · Voltage changes the capacitance

Changing VR changes W. Since C is approximately εA/W, the junction becomes a voltage-controlled capacitor.

Where is this useful? — Radio receivers
In a tuned radio receiver, the antenna picks up many electromagnetic waves at once. An LC resonant circuit can act as a resonant band-pass filter: signals near its resonant frequency are passed strongly, while signals far from that frequency are suppressed. A varactor gives the receiver an electronic way to move that resonant frequency. Changing VR changes Cj, and therefore changes f0 = 1/(2π√LCj). So instead of mechanically turning a variable capacitor, the radio can use a voltage to select a different channel.

In simple terms: antenna → many stations → LC filter selects one frequency → varactor voltage moves the selected station.
